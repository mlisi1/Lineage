What the plots show

Written by the report tool from the numbers of these two runs; the discussion behind it is in docs/decisions/0009-lifetime-learning.md.

  • Year 1 is the riskiest year. The lowest population of a seed's first year has a median of 40 creatures, against 75–86 in later years. Plants need months to cover half the land, so founders land only after a median of 8.6 months, a few months before their first winter: 40 animals of one species, at random ages, meet the winter dieback of the plants with no time to grow.
  • Later dips are winter dips, and they rarely reach zero. In later years the lowest point falls in December to February in 68% of seed-years. Herds go into winter larger, and strangers keep arriving with other genomes, a rescue effect; the 10th percentile of yearly minima is 11–24 creatures, and 3% of seed-years see an extinction.
  • Learning works, but buys little food. The diet fit (learned plant preference against the local plants) has a final-year median of +0.29, positive in 100% of seeds: each grazer learns which plant form is safe where it lives. Yet newborns in the forked worlds eat only +5.6% more per hour of youth (positive in 80% of seeds). On a balanced island the local difference between plant forms is worth a few percent of a bite; stronger toxins would make it larger but starve the predators. Eaten ÷ burnt barely moves (+1.2%): learners eat more and also live longer, so they burn more.
  • Evolution keeps learning cheap. The learning-rate gene is selected down (0.25 against 0.37 drifting with learning off); exploration is selected up (0.65 against 0.52 drifting with learning off); copying the mother's diet (SOCIAL) is neither favoured nor disfavoured (0.66 against 0.65 drifting). Exploration pays by itself (it spreads grazers over more pasture); a high learning rate costs more than the little food it wins. As Ackley & Littman found, once inherited behaviour is good enough, genes for learning lose value (shielding).
  • Learning shields the genes. Plant eaters' inherited bias toward low growth ends at +0.069 with learning on and +0.183 with it off (positive in 75% and 80% of seeds), while the learners' learned bias is +0.096. Without learning, selection writes the diet rule into the genes; with learning, creatures pick it up during life, so selection on the genes relaxes: the opposite of a Baldwin effect, which Ackley & Littman call shielding.
  • No Baldwin trend in 3 years. Time to competence falls over generations in 50% of seeds with learning on, 55% with it off. That is no better than the learning-off run, so it is not evidence of a Baldwin effect: neither curve clearly falls. A Baldwin effect needs learning to pay first and then many generations (in Ackley & Littman's world, millions of steps); 3 years give about 43.
  • Predators boom and bust. Predator numbers cycle in both runs: hunters arrive, multiply on the herds, and crash. Seeds have at least three predators in 60% of the time after year 1 with learning on, 71% off; in the last half year the median is 12 with learning on against 16 off, and kills per seed are 2391 against 2408. Learning prey are not harder to catch: in 30-day forks from the same worlds, kills and the movement and spacing of grazers do not change (ADR 0009). The gap builds over months at the level of populations, and the richer meat of this configuration only partly offsets it.
  • Learning has a modest running cost. A whole sim-day takes 308 ms with learning on and 282 ms off; most of the cost of M5 is the plant-form senses, which every creature computes either way.
