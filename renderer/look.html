Lineage look sheet

How the renderer draws the world, and what there is to choose. World: …. Renderer build ….

The world is a continent, and the screen shows it from three distances. The map view holds all of it. The middle view is four times closer: a region, its woods and shores, animals as small bodies. The local view is four times closer again and is about one animal: it and what it walks through fill the screen. All three are painted from the same cells by the same rules, and the plants of the middle view stand in the same places in the local one, drawn larger and in more detail.

Art pixels are 2 screen pixels: every picture marked full size is drawn at 512×300 and shown at 1024×600, pixel for pixel what the 7″ screen will show. Everything is drawn by the renderer spike (R0); plants and animals are drawn by rule, which is what lets the same plant be drawn at two distances, and can be redrawn by hand once the look is fixed.

This page is live (drawn with …): the creatures walk. npm run look writes a copy to reports/R0-look-sheet/.

A copy to read anywhere, generated by npm run look in renderer/; do not edit by hand. The pictures with animals in them are short loops, and the animals jump back when a loop restarts. The live page (npm run dev there, then /look.html) runs without end.

1. The map view settled

The whole continent, 2 art pixels to the cell: heights and cover interpolated between cells, each animal a dot in its own colour (your M3).

2. The middle view settled

A sixteenth of the continent, 8 art pixels to the cell. As you chose: the ground interpolated and dithered (G4), the large plants (P3), animals as outlined body plans (C3). The animals are smaller again: 0.5 of the first size instead of 0.85, so an animal of mass 1 is 4 to 5 art pixels long here and the heaviest about 11. That number is the same in every view; section 3 has it at three values to choose from.

3. The local view reworked

One animal from close to the ground, 32 art pixels to the cell (your L2): 16 by 9 cells in sight. Nothing is rescaled between the views: a tree is three cells tall and an animal half a cell long in the middle view too. Four things changed since the last sheet, each with proposals to choose from.

The ground: larger patches new

Why there were so many spots: the world's cells differ one from the next (grass, then a cell of scrub, then bare), which reads as texture when a cell is 8 pixels and as a patchwork when it is 32. On top of that the soil showed through the growth in blobs half a cell wide. Now a close view reads what grows over a few cells around, so a kind of ground only shows where it holds several cells together, and thin growth opens in gaps a cell or two across. The blending is as it was. The same meadow, read over one, two and three cells around, without plants or animals:

What the pixels are spent on instead is what the ground is made of. Each kind has its own detail, drawn by rule: blades and a bloom in grass, pebbles, grit and cracks in soil, ripples in sand, leaf litter under broadleaves and needles under conifers, ash where it burnt. Lichen is no longer a colour of the ground but rosettes on it, grey-green, mustard and chalk-white, thick where it grows thick, and scattered on any bare rock. A piece of each, enlarged twice:

Trees and bushes: three proposals new

The old trees had two faults in the drawing itself: the crown was cut off flat along its lower edge, and each tree carried its own see-through shadow, so that shadows darkened the crowns behind them and each other. Shadows are now painted on the ground, once, however many trees throw them, and crowns end where their leaves end.

For the style I looked at how pixel-art trees are usually built and wrote three rules after what I found. Each draws every tree and bush at any size, in three builds (as the scale has it, slender, broad) and with a different arrangement for every drawing. Rows: broadleaf grown ×3 and young ×2, conifer the same; then bushes (the second in berry) and, last, the three kinds at the middle view's size.

References: Slynyrd's Pixelblog 44 (top-down trees built from one bundle of leaves, layered in tones) and 15 (how much leaf detail a crown bears); the usual construction of top-down game trees, crown over a short trunk with the shadow apart; Gentree, which grows a tree by forking branches and hangs a clump of leaves on each end.

All three: a hunter in the densest wood, with company. Trees in front of the animal the picture is about go see-through while it is behind them.

What grows and lies among them new

Small plants, placed by what covers each cell. In grass: tufts to walk through, herbs, and four kinds of flower in five colours, which come in drifts (one kind and colour over several cells, thick in places, rare between). Under bushes and trees: ferns, mushrooms, fallen logs. On bare ground: stones and boulders, some grown over with lichen or moss; reeds on the shore; charred stumps where it burnt.

4. Creatures up close

Style C3, as chosen. Three things changed. Shading: every mass of the body now has its far side in shade (a cooler, darker tone) as well as a lit back, the largest a gleam; and every marking large enough is shaded the same way, each on its own. Markings stay on the body: the artifacts you saw were markings sticking out over the outline, or a highlight larger than what it lay on; each marking is now fitted to the mass it is on. Legs turn into fins with the aquatic gene (see its row below).

5. What it costs

The board is not on the desk, so this is an estimate: the three views measured on the development PC and scaled to the board's processor. An Allwinner H618 scores about 150 in Geekbench 6 on one core and this PC about 3,150: the board is some 21 times slower per core. It is another architecture and another graphics stack, so read the last column as an order of magnitude.

On the PC At the board's speed Against
Script per frame, map view (216 animals as dots) 0.2 ms 3 ms (up to 8) 33 ms a frame at 30 fps
Script per frame, middle view (50 animals) 0.2 ms 3 ms (up to 8)
Script per frame, middle view (all 216 animals alive) 0.5 ms 8 ms (up to 15)
Script per frame, local view (following one animal) 0.2 ms 1 ms (up to 8)
The page's process, any view 5 to 7 % of a core at 60 fps about 0.6 of a core at 30 fps 4 cores, of which the simulation may use ¼
Painting the map's and the middle view's ground 0.5 s 10 s once a sim-day
Painting the local view's ground, per piece of 8 × 8 cells 35 ms 0.6 s two or three new pieces whenever the animal has walked 8 cells
Pixels to fill per frame 512×300, two to three layers deep: about 0.4 million a Mali-G31 MP2, if Chromium is given it
WebGL drawn by the processor instead 2 to 3 cores out of reach 4 cores, 21 times slower
Chromium's memory about 0.6 GB 1 to 4 GB by model; simulation and server get 0.3

What follows from it:

  • Drawing the frames fits in all three views, with room. The local view is the cheapest per frame: few animals are in sight.
  • Painting ground is the one thing that does not fit in a frame. On the board a piece of the local view's ground would stop the picture for half a second, and the daily repaint of the other two for ten. Both have to be painted in the background (a worker), ahead of the camera for the local view. Measured as it is, the local view drops to 2 frames a second while a piece is painted.
  • Everything still hangs on the graphics driver. With the GPU the picture is small work; without it nothing here is fast enough. Reports for this board show the driver loading while Chromium still draws in software, so this is the first thing to try when the board is on the desk.
  • A 1 GB board is tight next to the simulation; 2 GB or more is safer.

Measured again with this sheet's local view: its calmer ground, detail and shadows cost about a fifth more to paint per piece than the ground before them (33 ms against 27), and the shading of the animals nothing that shows, since they are drawn smaller. "At the board's speed" is the same page with its processor throttled 21 times in Chromium; in brackets, the slowest frame in a hundred. The page's process and the memory are Chromium's own figures on the PC. Sources for the scores: Geekbench 6 uploads for the Orange Pi Zero 3 (same chip) and for the Core Ultra 9 275HX.

To choose, and to say

  • The ground's patches: K1, K2 or K3.
  • Trees and bushes: T1, T2 or T3, or what to take from each.
  • The size of the animals: A1, A2 or A3. One number for all three views.
  • The small plants and the ground's detail: whether this is the amount you had in mind, what is missing, what is too much.
  • Fins: whether legs webbing into flippers reads well, or should go further (a fluke, a fin down the back).
