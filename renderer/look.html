How the renderer draws the world, and what there is to choose. World: …. Renderer build ….

The world is a continent, and the screen shows it from three distances. The map view holds all of it. The middle view is four times closer: a region, its woods and shores, animals as small bodies. The local view is four times closer again and is about one animal: it and what it walks through fill the screen. All three are painted from the same cells by the same rules, and the plants of the middle view stand in the same places in the local one, drawn larger and in more detail.

Art pixels are 2 screen pixels: every picture marked full size is drawn at 512×300 and shown at 1024×600, pixel for pixel what the 7″ screen will show. Everything is drawn by the renderer spike (R0); plants and animals are drawn by rule, which is what lets the same plant be drawn at two distances, and can be redrawn by hand once the look is fixed.

Trees and bushes mended

What I found looking at them pixel by pixel, and changed:

  • A conifer's trunk stood a quarter of the way in from its left edge, not under its top: the rule that made it thinner than a broadleaf's also moved it. It is under the middle now, in every view.
  • Blocky leaves. A crown was some twenty large bundles, each with a saw-toothed rim, flat tones and a thick line of shade beneath. It is now about twice as many smaller bundles with soft rims; leaves are small slanted dabs that ruffle the line between two tones, and the shade between bundles is a thin line.
  • Conifers were split down the middle, light on the left and dark on the right with a straight line between. Each tier is now lit on its upper left and shaded under its right hem, needles hanging in the tones; the tips of a tier hang to different lengths and not under those of the next.
  • Bushes were cut off flat where a bundle reached below the ground line, or floated above their shadow. They end at the ground and sit on it.

Broadleaf grown ×3 and young ×2; conifers the same; bushes (the second in berry) and, last, the three kinds at the middle view's size. Three builds each: as the scale has it, slender, broad.

How large the animals are: six sizes to choose

You were right about the tree: the one beside the animals last time was a young conifer, 56 art pixels tall, so every size looked larger than it is against the wood. Here the trees and bushes are full grown: a broadleaf and a conifer 96 art pixels tall (three cells), a bush 40. They stand in a row on a meadow, and in front of them the same five animals at each size: masses 0.25, 0.7, 2, 5.7 and 16, from the lightest a genome allows to the heaviest. Most animals of this world weigh between 0.7 and 2.6. Nothing but the animals' size changes from one picture to the next.

What each size does to the middle view. So far one number sizes the animals in every view, which is what keeps the views in proportion. The middle view you found good is at 0.5, where an animal of mass 1 is 4½ art pixels long; at 0.35 it is 3, at 0.25 two, which is a dot with an outline. 4. Creatures up close mended

Style C3, shaded, with legs that turn into fins, as on the last sheet. Looking at every specimen and marking four times enlarged, this is what was wrong and is changed:

  • Ragged snouts. Tusks were drawn over the head without an outline, which left a fringe of stray pixels at the snout. They now come out from under the head, outlined with it.
  • Crabs trailed a club behind the carapace (a joint of the chain that should not show), carried a dark slot on the back (armour plates meant for long bodies) and had triangles for claws. A crab now ends with its carapace and has pincers: a palm and two fingers that close toward each other.
  • Markings. Bars were two crossing strokes per place, a zigzag; they are one slanted bar each, as a tiger's. Bands were fat ovals that read as blobs; they are narrow ovals across the back. A stripe down a back was a row of separate rectangles; it is ovals that run into each other. A fade on a shell was two spots; it is ovals nested toward the rear. A stripe on a shell was doubled by its own lit patch; long markings carry none now. A saddle's mask sat behind the eyes as a third blob; it lies across them.
  • Fins. A shelled animal with a little of the aquatic gene grew a sliver of tail fin under its shell: only bodies with a tail carry one now. Webbed feet were triangles that read as spikes; they are oval paddles, little wider than a foot. Flippers on a body that keeps other legs (a crab's, a beetle's last pair) are shorter.

5. What it costs

The board is not on the desk, so this is an estimate: the three views measured on the development PC and scaled to the board's processor. An Allwinner H618 scores about 150 in Geekbench 6 on one core and this PC about 3,150: the board is some 21 times slower per core. It is another architecture and another graphics stack, so read the last column as an order of magnitude.

On the PC At the board's speed Against
Script per frame, map view (216 animals as dots) 0.2 ms 3 ms (up to 8) 33 ms a frame at 30 fps
Script per frame, middle view (50 animals) 0.2 ms 3 ms (up to 8)
Script per frame, middle view (all 216 animals alive) 0.5 ms 8 ms (up to 15)
Script per frame, local view (following one animal) 0.2 ms 1 ms (up to 8)
The page's process, any view 5 to 7 % of a core at 60 fps about 0.6 of a core at 30 fps 4 cores, of which the simulation may use ¼
Painting the map's and the middle view's ground 0.5 s 10 s once a sim-day
Painting the local view's ground, per piece of 8 × 8 cells 35 ms 0.6 s two or three new pieces whenever the animal has walked 8 cells
Pixels to fill per frame 512×300, two to three layers deep: about 0.4 million a Mali-G31 MP2, if Chromium is given it
WebGL drawn by the processor instead 2 to 3 cores out of reach 4 cores, 21 times slower
Chromium's memory about 0.6 GB 1 to 4 GB by model; simulation and server get 0.3

What follows from it:

  • Drawing the frames fits in all three views, with room. The local view is the cheapest per frame: few animals are in sight.
  • Painting ground is the one thing that does not fit in a frame. On the board a piece of the local view's ground would stop the picture for half a second, and the daily repaint of the other two for ten. Both have to be painted in the background (a worker), ahead of the camera for the local view. Measured as it is, the local view drops to 2 frames a second while a piece is painted.
  • Everything still hangs on the graphics driver. With the GPU the picture is small work; without it nothing here is fast enough. Reports for this board show the driver loading while Chromium still draws in software, so this is the first thing to try when the board is on the desk.
  • A 1 GB board is tight next to the simulation; 2 GB or more is safer.

Measured again with this sheet's local view: its calmer ground, detail and shadows cost about a fifth more to paint per piece than the ground before them (33 ms against 27), and the shading of the animals nothing that shows, since they are drawn smaller. "At the board's speed" is the same page with its processor throttled 21 times in Chromium; in brackets, the slowest frame in a hundred. The page's process and the memory are Chromium's own figures on the PC. Sources for the scores: Geekbench 6 uploads for the Orange Pi Zero 3 (same chip) and for the Core Ultra 9 275HX.
